Proposed Solution — Remote Sensing + Chemometrics 
+ AI
We combine ESA Copernicus Sentinel-2 multispectral satellite imagery 
with chemometric feature engineering and AI regression to estimate 
soil properties without physical sampling at every point. Sentinel-2 is a 
pair of Earth-observation satellites that capture reflected light across 
visible, red-edge, near-infrared, and short-wave infrared bands at 10–20 
m spatial resolution with a 5-day revisit cycle, making it well suited for 
monitoring field-scale variability over time.
Chemometrics means extracting quantitative, physically meaningful 
predictors from spectral data. Instead of using raw pixels alone, we 
derive indices and band ratios that are sensitive to soil background, 
moisture, organic matter, and clay/mineral composition. Key features 
include BSI (Bare Soil Index) to highlight exposed soil, NDRE 
(Normalized Difference Red Edge) to capture subtle vegetation/soil 
stress signals where partial cover exists, and Clay Ratio to improve 
sensitivity to clay-related spectral absorption in SWIR bands.
The AI layer uses supervised regression models such as XGBoost and 
LightGBM. These models learn the mapping from spectral features and 
indices to ground-truth soil chemistry measurements from calibration 
samples. After training, the model predicts continuous values for 
nutrients and soil characteristics at unsampled locations, producing 
high-resolution 2D fertility heatmaps and variable-rate fertilizer 
dosage schedules delivered through a web GIS interface.

Literature Foundations — Machine Learning & Spatial 
Modeling
Ensemble Models Lead
Kammerlander et al. (2025) and Vohland et al. (2018) 
benchmark tree-based ensembles (Random Forest, XGBoost) 
outperform neural nets on tabular spectral data when 
properly engineered.
Feature & Validation Strategies
Meta-analyses and methodological papers emphasize feature 
selection, moisture normalization, PCA/indices, and robust 
spatial cross-validation to avoid overfitting and inflated 
metrics.
Hyperparameter Tuning
Strong performance depends on careful tuning of model 
complexity, learning rate, depth, and regularization. Grid 
search, randomized search, Bayesian optimization, and nested 
cross-validation are commonly recommended to balance 
accuracy with generalization, especially when sample sizes 
are modest and spatial autocorrelation can make naive tuning 
overly optimistic.
Class Imbalance in Soil Data
Soil datasets often overrepresent common classes or 
concentration ranges while rare soil types and extremes are 
under-sampled. Prior work supports stratified sampling, class 
weights, focal loss, resampling strategies, and threshold 
adjustment to improve minority-class recall without 
degrading calibration or spatial realism.
Transfer Learning & Domain Adaptation
When local field data are limited, transfer learning from 
larger spectral libraries or related regions can improve 
robustness. Reusing pretrained representations, calibrating 
with local samples, and adapting across sensors or landscapes 
help reduce labeling burden while addressing domain shift in 
illumination, soil background, and acquisition conditions.
Uncertainty Quantification
For mapping and decision support, point predictions are not 
enough. Ensemble variance, quantile regression, conformal 
prediction, and Bayesian approaches provide uncertainty 
estimates that expose unreliable predictions, support risk-
aware interpretation, and highlight where additional sampling 
would be most valuable.

Known Limitations & 
Methodological Pitfalls
1
Wet-Chemical Testing Constraints
Lab tests are time-intensive (2–4 weeks), costly (₹500–₹2,500/sample), spatially 
sparse (composite samples erase intra-field variability), and 
destructive/manual.
2
Naive Satellite Model Risks
Vegetation cover confounds soil signals; naive assumptions ignore sensor 
physics (P/K depend on mineral covariance); random splits cause spatial 
leakage and inflated R²; many studies stop short of farmer advisory 
translation.
3
Atmospheric Effects on Satellite Data
Clouds, haze, aerosols, water vapor, and variable illumination can distort 
reflectance values and shift spectral indices. Even after correction, residual 
atmospheric noise can obscure subtle soil-surface differences, especially in 
humid or cloudy regions and during narrow acquisition windows.
4
Calibration Drift Over Time
Sensor aging, platform transitions, and changing acquisition conditions can 
introduce calibration drift that weakens model reliability across seasons and 
years. Without periodic re-calibration against fresh ground truth, apparent 
trends may reflect instrument instability rather than genuine changes in soil 
properties.
5
Weak Transferability Across Regions
Models trained in one agro-ecological zone often fail elsewhere because soil 
mineralogy, texture, crop residue, management history, and climate differ 
substantially. This creates strong domain-shift effects, making local retraining, 
site-specific validation, and region-aware stratification essential before 
deployment.

Problem Statement & Research Challenges
Conventional laboratory soil testing is financially inaccessible, slow, and spatially sparse for smallholders, so most farmers make fertilizer 
decisions with incomplete information. The result is indiscriminate nutrient application, lower yields, avoidable input costs, and long-term soil 
degradation. Across smallholder farming systems, this challenge affects millions of farmers and has a direct economic impact through wasted 
fertilizer spend, reduced productivity, and missed opportunities for precision nutrient management.
This project asks three core research questions: (1) Can Sentinel-2 imagery be used to reliably estimate topsoil N, P, K, SOC, and pH at field-
relevant 10m resolution? (2) Which spectral, spatial, and terrain-related signals are most predictive across highly variable cropping conditions? 
(3) How can predictions be translated into an automated, cloud-native advisory pipeline that is robust enough for operational use by farmers 
and agronomists?
Model Secondary Covariance
Isolate Bare Soil
Resolve Depth Paradox
The technical hurdles are substantial: soil properties are not directly visible from space, vegetation cover obscures the signal, and nutrient 
responses vary by soil type, season, and management history. The model must also handle limited ground truth, avoid spatial leakage during 
validation, generalize across fields and agro-ecological zones, and produce outputs that are interpretable and actionable rather than just 
statistically accurate.

System Design & Pipeline 
Overview
The system is organized as a four-stage cloud-native pipeline that turns Sentinel-2 
imagery into agronomic recommendations for smallholder fields. It begins with 
scalable data ingestion, moves through pixel-level filtering and feature 
engineering, trains multi-target machine learning models, and ends with spatially 
explicit nutrient and fertilizer advisory outputs.
1) Ingestion: A STAC API (SpatioTemporal Asset Catalog) is used to query imagery 
by time, location, platform, and cloud cover, allowing the pipeline to stream only 
the Sentinel-2 L2A scenes intersecting each farmer polygon. The imagery is 
accessed as COGs (Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFFs), which are geospatial raster files 
structured for efficient HTTP range requests. That means the pipeline can read only 
the needed tiles and bands without downloading entire scenes, which is critical for 
scalable, serverless processing.
2) Filtering: The raw scenes are filtered to remove unusable pixels and build a 
representative bare-soil composite. First, the SCL (Scene Classification Layer) is 
used to mask clouds, cirrus, cloud shadows, water, and saturated or invalid pixels. 
Then vegetation-contaminated observations are down-weighted or excluded using 
spectral indices such as NDVI and BSI. NDVI helps identify green vegetation, while 
BSI highlights exposed soil. A median compositing strategy across the remaining 
valid observations reduces residual noise from haze, mixed pixels, and outliers, 
producing a more stable bare-soil signal for each field.
3) AI Modeling: For each composite pixel or field sample, the system performs 
feature engineering from the Sentinel-2 spectral stack. It extracts the 10 m bands 
and computes additional indices and transforms, such as vegetation, soil, and 
moisture-sensitive features, yielding a richer predictor set than raw reflectance 
alone. These features are then fed into multi-target ensemble models trained to 
estimate topsoil properties including N, P, K, SOC, and pH. Spatial GroupKFold is 
used so that neighboring samples are kept together during cross-validation, 
reducing spatial leakage and providing a more realistic estimate of generalization 
to unseen areas.
4) Advisory: The model outputs are converted into georeferenced 10 m nutrient 
rasters, which are then aggregated into field-level management zones and 
prescription layers. The advisory layer can produce zonation maps showing 
relative deficiency or sufficiency, plus fertilizer split recommendations such as 
Urea, DAP, and MOP allocations. Outputs are typically delivered as map-ready 
raster tiles, polygon summaries, and tabular recommendations so they can support 
both digital visualization and practical on-farm decision-making.
